### A2. The superseded workflow — NOW RESOLVED, and the answer has a twist

The research asserts that each MP's ₹5 Cr is *"released upfront (in two ₹2.5 cr tranches)"* and that on sanction there is *"an automatic fund transfer to the district's holding account in PFMS."* Your handwritten notes say the same thing — *"Money goes to DA i.e. DM or Collector → RBI accounts/Government Account"*, *"DM enters, verifies... releases the initial 2.5cr"*, *"DM office hires contractors, pays the bills"*. **That is the pre-2023 model.**

The same notes, three inches away, also say *"Central Nodal Agency — fundamental governing body managing the flow."* **Those two descriptions are mutually exclusive and were never reconciled.**

Part F item 8 resolves it: MPLADS adopted the **TSA Hybrid / Model 1A** fund-flow arrangement **from 1 April 2025**, running through the e-SAKSHI/PFMS ecosystem with SBI generating the consolidated daily fund requirement, and with Implementing Agency registration flowing through PFMS before it reaches e-SAKSHI. Funds are held centrally and released just-in-time rather than parked with the District Authority in advance — *this last clause is an inference from what "TSA Model 1A" means, not a sentence in the source, so get the clause before you assert it* (see §0).

Your dataset spans all of this and has **no field recording which regime a work belongs to**. A rule that reasons about installments is therefore not simply right or wrong; it is right for one cohort and wrong for another. ### A4. The hedged claims — resolved, with two that turned out to hide real bugs

**Confirmed by Part F, and safe to state:**

**The 45-day window** (item 1). The DA must **sanction or reject** within 45 days **from receipt of the MP's recommendation**. Two details matter more than the number:

- *The clock start is the recommendation date, and your code gets this right.* `sla_engine.ts:31-33` keys off `work.recommended_date`, not `sanction_date`. - *"Or reject" creates a false-positive class you have not guarded — and the repo disagrees with itself about it.* A recommendation rejected inside 45 days is **compliant**. `sla_engine.ts` selects works on `.is('sanction_date', null)` with **no status filter**, so any work that is unsanctioned for a reason other than delay — rejected, withdrawn, cancelled — is counted as an SLA breach. But there is a **second, independent 45-day implementation** the rest of this audit missed: `routers/sla.ts:11-45` also defines `SLA_LIMIT_DAYS = 45` and *does* filter `.eq('status', 'PROPOSED')` at `:22`, and that is the endpoint behind the `/sla` screen. So the engine that writes alerts and the endpoint that draws the page apply different definitions of the same clause. On the seeded corpus the engine's omission happens not to bite, because the 43 works with a null `sanction_date` are exactly the 43 `PROPOSED` ones and the 39 `CANCELLED` ones all carry sanction dates. On real e-SAKSHI data, where rejection is a first-class outcome the guidelines explicitly provide for, it will bite immediately. **Add the status filter to `sla_engine`, reconcile the two implementations to one, and count rejections as a separate statistic** — "recommendations rejected within SLA" is itself a number a monitoring officer wants. This is no longer "an uncited number that a judge might ask about"; it is a number that an official document contradicts, displayed on a screen built to demonstrate conformance to it, labelled `docs/DATA_CONTRACT.md:35` as the "**MoSPI Published Delivery Rate**" and `docs/API_CONTRACT.md:50` as the "official 19.24% MoSPI benchmark", with `docs/DEMO_SCRIPT.md:9` claiming historical MoSPI data "**proves**" it. `HANDOFF.md:4`'s "Read date for all external figures: 2026-08-23" describes a verification that left no trace in the repository and evidently did not happen.

**Where the number most likely came from — and why the answer is flattering rather than damning.** 859 ÷ 4,466 = 19.2342%, so the pair is internally consistent and was not invented. Against the official totals, ₹4,466 Cr of sanctions is about two-thirds of the ₹6,680.29 Cr reached by January 2026 — consistent with **a real national snapshot taken roughly a year earlier**. Between that snapshot and the official one, sanctioned value grew **1.50×** while completed value grew **3.94×**. *(This reconstruction is a hypothesis: the 4,466/859 pair is still unsourced, and I cannot confirm its date or scope.)* But if it is right, then the most probable story is not fabrication — it is that **someone recorded a true figure and it went stale**, and it went stale in a very specific way that proves the point of the next section.

Note also that **22.49%** is completed-value ÷ *authorised* value (₹3,387.38 / ₹15,062.49). So "the completion rate" has at least three defensible definitions — 50.71% against sanctions, 61.88% by count, 22.49% against authorisations — differing by more than a factor of two. **Whatever number you display, display its denominator beside it.** Half of this entire section exists because nobody wrote the denominator down.

**The blast radius is larger than the docs suggest.** The literal appears on **nineteen lines across ten files** — twenty if you count the companion `percentage = 19.3` sitting on the adjacent line, and twenty *occurrences* because `DEMO_SCRIPT.md:9` carries it twice. Two of the nineteen are places you would not think to grep: `supabase/full_schema.sql:304` and `supabase/migrations/001_initial_schema.sql:303` declare `target_completion_rate DOUBLE PRECISION NOT NULL DEFAULT 0.1924`, so the wrong figure is baked into the schema and will survive a code-only fix. A third is `frontend/src/components/ui.tsx:126-130`, a reusable "19.24% MoSPI Meter" gauge whose **default prop is `target = 19.24`**, so any future use of that component silently inherits it — its single caller today, `OverviewPage.tsx:336`, does pass the value explicitly. The rest: `calibration.ts:5,13`; `CalibrationPage.tsx:27,34,69`; `OverviewPage.tsx:185,332,336`; `ui.tsx:338`; `README.md:10,69`; `DEMO_SCRIPT.md:3,9`; `DATA_CONTRACT.md:35`; `API_CONTRACT.md:50`. Three further lines — `calibration.ts:23,24,44` — consume the constant by name rather than repeating the literal, so fixing `:13` carries them; but `:23` is worth reading anyway, because `corpusRate` **falls back to `TARGET_COMPLETION_RATE` itself when `totalSanctioned` is zero**, which makes an empty database report perfect agreement with the benchmark at 0% deviation. A calibration screen that shows conformance when it has no data is the same class of error as the fake copilot.

**And one of those sites now argues against itself.** `CalibrationPage.tsx:69` reads: *"Synthetic test datasets in governance systems often generate unrealistic completion figures (e.g. 70–80%), which causes anomaly detectors to fail when exposed to real-world government data. Our dataset generator is hard-calibrated to preserve the true 19.24% delivery ratio."* The real figure is **61.88% by count** — much nearer the "unrealistic" 70–80% it warns about than the 19.24% it calibrates to. The paragraph's reasoning is sound and its conclusion is inverted: the generator has been hard-calibrated **away** from reality by a factor of ~3, which means every threshold tuned on that corpus — the MAD z-score cut, the probation rate at 0.40, the alert budget of 10 per district — was tuned against a completion distribution that does not exist. That is a real technical debt, not just a wrong string.

**Problem two survives Part F intact, and can now be quantified.** A completion ratio computed over *all* sanctions counts every not-yet-due work as a shortfall. With the one-year deadline now confirmed (A4), the official window of 1 April 2023 – 22 January 2026 is **1,027 days**, and only works sanctioned on or before 22 January 2025 — the first **662 days, i.e. 64.46% of the window** — have passed their deadline. Assuming sanctioned value accrued uniformly across the window:

| Derived from Part F item 3 | Value basis | Count basis |
|---|---|---|
| Sanctioned stock past its one-year deadline | ≈ ₹4,306 Cr | ≈ 71,937 works |
| Completed | ₹3,387.38 Cr | 69,061 works |
| **Due-cohort completion rate** | **≈ 78.7%** | ≈ 96.0% — *see below, do not use* |
| **Overdue and incomplete** | **≈ ₹919 Cr** | ≈ 2,876 works |

**These are point estimates under one assumption, not bounds — and one of the two columns should be deleted.** Say the assumption out loud, because a judge will ask: sanctioned value is taken to accrue uniformly across the window. Relax it in the likely direction — 2023-24 was the first year of a brand-new portal following two suspended years, so sanctions almost certainly ramped up rather than accruing evenly — and the past-due share falls below 64.46%, which pushes the cohort rate **above** 78.7% and the overdue pool **below** ₹919 Cr. (An earlier draft of this audit called 78.7% an "upper bound" and ₹919 Cr a "lower bound" while simultaneously arguing the ramp-up moves them the other way. That was incoherent: they are estimates conditional on uniform accrual, and the ramp-up is a one-sided sensitivity on that condition, not a bound within it.)

**The count basis is degenerate and must not be shown.** 96.0% looks like a stronger version of the same story, but it is an artefact: the count-basis rate reaches exactly 100% when the past-due share of works falls to 61.88%, which is only 2.6 points below the uniform value of 64.46%. Any plausible amount of back-loading drives it above 100% — a nonsense result, and the giveaway that the estimator has no headroom on this basis. Report the value basis with its assumption stated, and drop the count column rather than defend it.

**The gap between the two columns is still the most useful signal here, handled carefully.** 78.7% by value against a count-basis rate that saturates means the incomplete works are the **big** ones. The direct evidence for the mechanism is weaker than that contrast suggests, though: the average sanctioned work is ₹5.99 lakh against an average *completed* work of ₹4.90 lakh, which is an 18% gap, not a dramatic one. The stronger corroboration is that the whole sanctioned-but-incomplete pool averages **₹7.74 lakh** per work (₹3,292.91 Cr ÷ 42,539) — 29% above the sanctioned average and 58% above the completed average. **The delivery problem is a large-works problem** — a targeting rule you can implement, a justification for weighting your alert budget by value rather than count, and derived entirely from official figures. State it as strongly supported, not as "confirmed directly."

**So here is what to actually do.** Replace 19.24% with 50.71% by value and 61.88% by count, cited to the Standing Committee report with its date range on the face of the screen — including in both SQL schema defaults and the `ui.tsx` gauge prop. Then compute the vintage-adjusted rate and show it *beside* the gross one. Recalibrate the generator to the real distribution, and re-tune the thresholds afterwards.

One implementation caveat, unchanged by Part F and still waiting to bite you: `works.completion_target_date` exists (`full_schema.sql:100`) but has **no writer anywhere in the repo** — absent from the 21-column ingest spec, absent from `worksToUpsert`, absent from the generator, and null in all three CSVs. Its only read (`WorkDetailPage.tsx:140`) already falls back to `'Standard 24 Months'`. Filtering on it naively restricts the denominator to **zero rows**. Derive the deadline as `sanction_date + 12 months` instead — and see B20, because on the current demo corpus that filter returns zero rows anyway for a completely different reason.

Then say the thing that is true, defensible, and now backed by arithmetic a judge can check:

> "The published aggregate cannot distinguish a work that is late from a work that is merely young. On the official figures, national completion reads 50.7% by value — but once you restrict the denominator to works whose one-year deadline has actually passed, delivery is around 79% by value and higher by count, and the genuinely overdue pool is roughly ₹900 crore, concentrated in large works. That distinction is the first thing a monitoring officer needs, and the current dashboard cannot give it to them."

That is a better pitch than the original in every respect. It is sourced. It is arithmetically checkable. It concedes the flattering part of the truth before anyone forces you to, which is exactly what buys you credibility for the unflattering part. And it still leaves you a ₹900 crore problem worth building a platform for — while telling you *which* works to point it at.

### A6. What the official figures say about where the scheme actually leaks — new, and it should reorder your roadmap

Part F item 3 gives four stages, not two. Reading them as a funnel is the single most useful thing in this audit, and nobody has done it yet. All figures below are derived from item 3's four numbers; the arithmetic is checkable in one line each.

| Stage | Value | Converts to next stage | Value stranded at this stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Authorised / released | ₹15,062.49 Cr | 88.53% | **₹1,727.44 Cr never recommended** |
| Recommended (2,28,761 works) | ₹13,335.05 Cr | 50.10% by value, 48.78% by count | **₹6,654.76 Cr recommended, never sanctioned** |
| Sanctioned (1,11,600 works) | ₹6,680.29 Cr | 50.71% by value, 61.88% by count | **₹3,292.91 Cr sanctioned, not complete** |
| Completed (69,061 works) | ₹3,387.38 Cr | — | — |

**The largest stalled pool in the scheme is recommendation-to-sanction: ₹6,654.76 Cr, roughly twice the sanctioned-but-incomplete pool.** Just under half of everything MPs recommend, by value and by count, never becomes a sanction. Your rule catalogue is almost entirely post-sanction — cost outliers, progress pacing, installments, UCs, photos, inspections. **The money is stuck one stage before any of it applies.**

How much of that ₹6,654.76 Cr is a genuine backlog rather than work-in-flight? At the observed recommendation run rate (₹13,335.05 Cr over 1,027 days ≈ **₹4,739 Cr/year**), a 45-day pipeline holds about **₹584 Cr** legitimately in-flight, leaving roughly **₹6,070 Cr sitting beyond the 45-day window**. But the guidelines say *sanction **or reject*** within 45 days, so that residue is **rejections and breaches mixed together, and the official figures do not separate them.** That is the single most valuable number in the scheme that nobody publishes, and asking for it is a much better question to put to a MoSPI panel than anything about completion rates: *"Of the recommendations not sanctioned within 45 days, how many were rejected on merit and how many simply expired?"* Note the same conflation is a bug in your code (A4, second bullet) — so you can say you found it in your own build before you found it in theirs. Apply the same in-flight discipline to the top row of the table: ₹1,727.44 Cr of unrecommended entitlement is about **133 days** of recommendations at that run rate, so call it *unrecommended*, not *idle*.

**Two operational loads, derived, that should change what you build.** Nationally this is about **223 recommendations per day**; across roughly 780 districts that is on average some **50 sanctions and around 100 recommendations per district per year**, each needing a feasibility check inside 45 days. And works under implementation are about 42,539 nationally, so the **10% inspection mandate is roughly 4,250 inspections a year nationally — single digits per district.**

Two caveats on those figures, because they are the ones a judge is most likely to probe. The district count of ~780 is **domain knowledge, not from Part F** — get the current figure before quoting it. And 42,539 is simply sanctioned-minus-completed, which includes cancelled, abandoned and on-hold works, so **4,250 is an upper bound** on the mandate; the true requirement is smaller. Both caveats push the same way, which is the useful direction: **the inspection requirement is not a capacity problem.** A district failing it is failing at record-keeping and evidence quality, not at volume. So the useful intervention is not "help them inspect more" — it is "make the evidence from the handful of inspections they already do trustworthy and searchable," which points at the document and photo work in Part D rather than at scheduling tools. *(All of these are national averages; a metro district serving several MPs will run well above them.)*

**The roadmap consequence.** P-01 — screening free-text recommendations for eligibility against the guidelines annexure, at the moment of recommendation — stops being one AI feature among several and becomes **the highest-value intervention available in this scheme**, because it is the only one that acts on the stage where ₹6,654.76 Cr is stranded. It is also the stage the incumbent's reporting treats as a single "pending beyond 45 days" count. Build that, and you are not competing with e-SAKSHI's reports; you are answering the question they raise.

## 4. PART C — The AI problem

### C0. First, the competitive fact that Part F surfaced

Before the AI question, a harder one. Part F item 6 records that e-SAKSHI **already generates reports** for matters *"such as"* — its wording, and the phrase matters:

- recommendations **pending beyond 45 days** — this is your SLA engine, and `SLAPage.tsx`
- sanctioned works **incomplete beyond the applicable period** — this is R-006 and R-018
- works with **no payment for extended periods** — this is R-014

At least three of your headline detectors are features of the system you are proposing to oversee, and "such as" means there may well be more of them in the live portal than the Standing Committee material enumerates. If a MoSPI judge says *"we already have that report"* about your first three demo screens, the demo is over regardless of how good the hash chain is. **Assume they will say it, and get there first.** Treat the list as a floor, not a ceiling: before any pitch that starts with "we do what they don't," get a walkthrough of the live portal's report menu and take a screenshot of everything in it.

The good news is that this is a positioning problem, not an architecture problem, and Part F item 10 hands you the framing. On **23 January 2026** MoSPI convened a national workshop with around 120 state and UT representatives, explicitly to gather feedback on *implementation challenges, timely completion, accountability, monitoring, and further e-SAKSHI functionality*. They are, on the record, looking for exactly this. And Part F item 9 records what is still broken **despite** the end-to-end digital system: MPs still facing operational and technical issues, **significant delays in project sanction**, and **discrepancies in constituency maps and names in the portal**. That is an official statement that threshold reports have not solved the sanction-delay problem.

So the line to draw is between **a threshold report and an explanation**:

> "e-SAKSHI can already tell an officer *which* recommendations have been pending past 45 days. It cannot tell them *why*, cannot tell them which of next month's files will breach, cannot read the recommendation to see whether it was ever eligible, and cannot distinguish a file rejected on merit from one that simply expired. The Committee's own record says sanction delays persist despite the portal. A count is not a diagnosis."

Everything in Part D that reads **unstructured input** — free text, scanned documents, photographs, speech — is untouched by anything the incumbent is documented as generating, because the documented reports look only at dates and amounts. That is not a coincidence; it is the boundary of what a threshold report can do. **Your defensible territory is exactly the Tier 1 list** — with the caveat that the "such as" wording means this territory is your defensible floor, and the incumbent's is possibly larger than the three named reports.

### C1. Now the AI problem itself

Stated plainly, because it is better to hear it here than from a judge: **this repository contains no artificial intelligence or machine learning of any kind.**

An exhaustive search for tensorflow, onnx, torch, sklearn, xgboost, lightgbm, IsolationForest, clustering, embeddings, any LLM provider or framework, tesseract/OCR, any computer-vision library, tf-idf, cosine similarity, regression, forecasting, `.fit(`, `.predict(` returns **nothing** — the only hits are the two places the code brags about *not* using IsolationForest (`cost_outlier.ts:4`, `util.ts:163`; a third is a line in `HANDOFF.md`, a document, and that file is deleted from the working tree). Backend dependencies are `express`, `cors`, `dotenv`, `yaml`, `supabase-js`. There is no model file, no weights, and **no outbound call to any model or inference service anywhere in the backend** — the only literal `https://` string in `backend/src` is a localhost CORS check (`server.ts:49`). (Be precise about that when you say it: the backend *does* make outbound HTTPS calls constantly, because the whole data layer is `@supabase/supabase-js` talking to a hosted Postgres. The claim is that none of that traffic goes to a model.)

What genuinely exists, categorised honestly: **real statistics** — median/MAD robust z-score (`util.ts:136-169`) and Dice token-set overlap (`util.ts:181-192`); **deterministic geometry** — Haversine (`util.ts:118`); **bit arithmetic** — Hamming distance (`util.ts:208`), a comparison primitive with no hash *producer* behind it; **cryptography** — a SHA-256 hash chain; and **date arithmetic and thresholds** for everything else, including all 19 rules.

Several of these are legitimately good engineering, and this deserves saying before the criticism lands. The MAD z-score is the right choice and Doctrine 7 defends it well — *"'3.4× the district median for this category' is followable by an officer."* **Keep that argument.** Explainable statistics beating a black box is a strong, mature position and you should make it out loud. The 2-of-3 corroboration gate on duplicate detection is genuinely good design — requiring independent signals to agree is exactly how you keep a fuzzy matcher from embarrassing an officer. The seeded PRNG makes the corpus reproducible. `tests/public_leakage.test.ts` really does enforce the Doctrine 4 whitelist. And the `verification_status` honesty contract is a better idea than most production govtech systems have. Four of those five are *implemented*, not merely documented.

The problem is not the absence of ML. **The problem is the branding written on top of it.** `README.md:4` calls DRISHTI an "AI & Cryptographic Oversight Engine." The `/` Overview screen renders a "DRISHTI AI Copilot" (`OverviewPage.tsx:438`) with a glowing orb, a "Live" pulse badge (`:440-442`), an "Ask DRISHTI anything…" input (`:507`) and an "Evaluating algorithmic telemetry…" spinner (`:484`). **Exit 1 — drop the AI framing.** Rename to "Rules & Statistical Oversight Engine," delete the copilot, and lean hard on explainability, the honesty contract, rule probation and the audit chain. This is coherent and defensible, but it competes weakly on a problem statement judged on AI.

**Exit 2 — build two or three real AI capabilities from Part D, and make the copilot honest.** A real text-to-SQL query layer over a read-only view *that shows the officer the generated query* is legitimate, buildable, demo-friendly, and turns your biggest liability into your best demo moment. Recommended. Start with P-15, P-01 and P-06.

Either way: **remove the `setTimeout` and the fake "Live" badge today.** They are the indefensible part.

## 5. PART D — Pain points in the authorities' workflow, and which are genuinely AI-solvable

Scope, per your brief: **District Authority, State Nodal Department, MoSPI central monitoring, and the field inspector** — the authorities. MP office and Implementing Agency appear only where they create work for an authority.

Each pain point is graded:

**Tier 1** — genuine AI/ML; no rule-based substitute exists, because the input is unstructured (free text, images, scanned documents, speech).
**Tier 2** — a rule exists but AI substantially raises its ceiling.
**Tier 3** — looks like AI in a pitch, is really deterministic. *Do not brand these as AI.* Listed to protect you in Q&A.

Coverage is judged against the current build.

### District Authority — the DM's office

**P-01 · Eligibility screening of free-text recommendations · Tier 1 · Gap — and now the highest-value item in the list**
An MP's recommendation arrives as prose: *"construction of community hall cum marriage hall near the Hanuman temple, Ward 7."* The nodal officer must decide whether that is an eligible work, and MPLADS guidelines carry a long annexure of eligible and ineligible items — religious structures, assets for individual benefit, commercial premises, land acquisition, memorials. This is a **text-understanding problem**, not a lookup, which is exactly why R-011 is dead: it checks a 15-value category dropdown, but the eligibility signal lives in the description the dropdown throws away. The AI form is a classifier or retrieval-augmented check over the guideline annexure returning *eligible / ineligible / needs human review* **with the specific clause quoted back**. That citation is what makes it usable by an officer and consistent with Doctrine 7.

**Part F strengthened this item twice.** Item 2 confirms the annexure is the authoritative source for proposal screening, *and* that eligibility "is determined by the principles and conditions of the scheme rather than by a single keyword-based classification." That sentence is simultaneously an endorsement of retrieval-with-clause-citation and a precise diagnosis of why R-011's dropdown check was never going to work — the guidelines themselves say the problem is not keyword-shaped. And A6 shows **₹6,654.76 Cr of recommendations never reach sanction**, roughly twice the sanctioned-but-incomplete pool, which makes the recommendation stage the largest value leak in the scheme and this the only pain point that acts on it.

Data needed: `title` + `description` (you have both) and the guidelines annexure as text (you do not — get it; it is now the single highest-value document to obtain).

**P-02 · Cost reasonableness against Schedule of Rates · Tier 1 (matching) + Tier 3 (comparison) · Partial**
The DA must check proposed cost against the state PWD Schedule of Rates. The hard part is not the comparison — it is that *"repair of village approach road, 1.2 km"* must be matched to SoR line items written in engineering nomenclature. That is **semantic matching over a large catalogue**, and it is why SoR integration is repeatedly described as hard. Once matched, the comparison is arithmetic (Tier 3). Your MAD z-score against district-and-category peers is a legitimate proxy and you should keep it — it needs no external data, which is a real advantage — but be precise in the pitch: **you detect works that are expensive *relative to peers*, not works that breach the SoR.** Those are different findings and an engineer in the room will know it.

**P-03 · Pre-sanction duplicate and overlap detection · Tier 2, high value · Partial, and improvable cheaply**
"Have we already sanctioned this?" Same asset proposed by two MPs, or re-proposed in a new financial year, or split across agencies. Your `duplicate.ts` does 2-of-3 corroboration on text + geo (500 m) + amount (15%) — the *architecture is right* and the corroboration requirement is genuinely good design. Two weaknesses, both cheap to fix. First, signal one (`tokenSetRatio`, `util.ts:181-192`) is a **Dice coefficient over token sets** — purely lexical. It will miss *"CC road"* versus *"cement concrete road"*, *"Anganwadi Kendra"* versus *"Anganwadi Centre"*, Hindi/English transliteration, and any paraphrase — which is most real duplicates. Second, it compares **titles only**; `description` is never used, discarding the richest text you have. Swapping in **multilingual sentence embeddings** over title *and* description, while keeping the 2-of-3 gate and keeping geo and amount as the corroborating signals, is a contained change that measurably improves recall without weakening explainability. Third improvement, free: **run it at recommendation time, not only post-hoc.** Catching a duplicate before sanction is worth far more than flagging one after payment.

**P-04 · Reading the paperwork — the most underrated pain point here · Tier 1 · Total gap**
Utilisation certificates, completion certificates, measurement books, bills and sanction letters arrive as scans and phone photographs. Someone in the DM's office opens each one and eyeballs whether the UC amount matches the sanction, whether dates are consistent, whether the signatory is authorised, whether the work described is the work sanctioned. **This is the single largest volume of pure drudgery in the whole workflow, and it is the most AI-native task in the scheme** — OCR plus key-value extraction plus cross-field consistency against the portal record, surfacing only mismatches. Your data model reduces this entire problem to `has_uc BOOLEAN` and a `uc_date` that nothing reads. Nobody else in a hackathon will be doing this, it is unambiguously AI, it needs no privileged access to build a demo, and every officer who has done the job will recognise it immediately. **If you build one new thing, consider this one.**

**P-05 · Queue triage under a real workload cap · Tier 2 · Partially covered, and your design is already good**
A nodal officer has dozens of pending items and time for a handful. Your alert budget (10 per district, overflow to a visible `BACKLOG`) and rule probation (auto-suspend below 40% actionable over 25 reviews) are **a genuinely thoughtful answer to alert fatigue** — keep both and present them as a feature. The honest AI extension is **not** a composite risk score; Doctrine 2 rightly forbids that. It is calibrating each rule's empirical precision from accumulated officer decisions, so ranking reflects *measured* reliability per rule and per district. That respects the doctrine and gets you learning behaviour. Note it needs `review_actions` volume you do not yet have — so present it as designed-and-instrumented, not as running.

**P-06 · Evidence photo verification · Tier 1 · Gap (the one existing piece is dead)**
Before releasing payment the DA must satisfy themselves that the photo shows the claimed asset, finished. Five distinct checks hide inside "verify the photo," and they are not the same problem:

*Geotag present and inside the project area* — Tier 3, pure EXIF parsing. Do it, do not call it AI.
*Same photo reused across works* — perceptual hashing. Honest label: **computer vision, not machine learning.** Your comparison primitive exists; the hasher does not, and `works.evidence_image_key` — the field the detector actually reads — has **zero writers anywhere in the repo** (B5). Fix the pipeline before claiming the capability.
*Does the image show the asset type claimed* — **Tier 1.** A "community hall" that is a photograph of a shopfront. Image classification over asset categories.
*Does it show a completed asset or a foundation* — **Tier 1, harder.** Progress-stage estimation is where the real money leaks, because payment is gated on completion.
*Is the image manipulated, re-photographed from a screen, or synthetically generated* — **Tier 1, and the most contemporary angle available to you.** In 2026 an implementing agency can generate a convincing photograph of a completed road. No scheme guideline anticipates this, and geotag rules do not catch it. Raising it demonstrates you understand where this threat model is actually going, and it differentiates you sharply.

**Part F item 7 adds a sixth consideration that is worth more than the other five combined.** The March 2026 release states that e-SAKSHI now allows stakeholders to upload photographs *"including corrected photographs where an incorrect image had previously been uploaded."* Two things follow. First, a defensive one: **photo replacement is now a sanctioned, routine operation**, so a naive reuse-or-change detector will flag legitimate corrections as anomalies — your detector must treat "image changed" as normal and reason about the *sequence* instead. Second, and much more important: it raises the question **whether the superseded image is retained.** If a correction overwrites the original, then the evidence of record for a payment decision is **mutable**, and there is an officially documented path by which the photograph that justified a payment can be replaced after the fact.

That is the strongest justification your append-only SHA-256 ledger will ever have, and you did not know it was there. Right now the hash chain is defended in the abstract — "tamper-evident audit trail" — which sounds like table stakes. Instead: *"the portal permits an uploaded photograph to be corrected. We hash every image at submission and chain it, so the image that justified a payment can be produced later even if it has since been replaced. We are not alleging misuse; we are removing the need to trust that it did not happen."* That is a specific, sourced, non-accusatory argument for your core cryptographic feature, aimed at a real documented mechanism rather than a hypothetical attacker. **Confirm the retention behaviour before asserting it** — if e-SAKSHI does version images, say so and present your hashing as independent corroboration instead, which is still a good argument.

**P-07 · Multilingual free text · Tier 1 · Gap**
Recommendations, agency remarks, inspector notes and citizen complaints arrive in regional languages and Roman transliteration. Every text feature above degrades or fails on this. Any text pipeline needs multilingual normalisation as its first stage — worth stating explicitly because it shows you have thought past an English-only demo, and it is the most common unexamined assumption in Indian govtech prototypes.

### Field inspector / Junior Engineer

**P-08 · Which works to inspect · Tier 2 · Gap**
Inspection is a mandated percentage, so the binding question is *which* ones, and today the answer is convenience and proximity. The right formulation is **maximise expected detection per travel-kilometre** — risk-informed sampling combined with geographic clustering so one trip covers several suspicious works. You have `latitude`/`longitude` and an `inspections` table, so this is feasible now. Deliberately include a small random sample alongside the risk-selected ones, or the model only ever validates its own priors — a point worth making aloud, because it shows statistical care.

**P-09 · On-site capture · Tier 1 · Gap (`idb` is a dependency; no offline queue exists)**
An inspector standing at a site with poor connectivity fills a checklist on a phone. Two AI wins: **speech-to-structured-form in the local language**, so observations are dictated rather than typed; and **on-device photo QC before they leave** — blurry, no geotag, doesn't resemble the claimed asset. That second one is worth more than it sounds, because the alternative to catching a bad photo on site is a repeat visit that will not happen.

**P-10 · Reconciling inspector evidence against agency claims · Tier 1 · Gap**
You store agency photos and `inspections.photo_keys` separately and never compare them. Cross-source comparison of the same asset — agency claim versus independent inspector observation — is the strongest integrity signal available anywhere in this scheme, because it is two parties with different incentives photographing one object.

### State Nodal Department

**P-11 · Where to intervene, and on whom · Tier 2 · Gap**
The state officer's real question is not "which works are late" but "**is this district slow, or does it simply have harder works?**" Answering it needs time-to-event modelling of sanction → completion with district and agency effects, controlling for work type and size. Without that control you punish districts for their portfolio mix, which destroys officer trust faster than any false positive. Your heatmap shows raw day counts, which cannot separate these.

**P-12 · Implementing-agency performance profiling · Tier 2 · Gap, and the cheapest real win available**
`AgenciesPage.tsx` **makes zero API calls** and renders hardcoded numbers ("14.2 Mo", "DRDA 88.4% timely"). Yet you have `agency_id`, dates and amounts — everything needed to compute agency-level completion time properly. And note this is **doctrine-safe**: Doctrine 3 bars *MP*-level aggregation, and says nothing about agencies — which is fortunate, because **the agency is where execution accountability actually sits.** Model expected completion time from work type and size, then rank agencies by deviation from their own expectation. This is a real screen replacing a fake one.

### MoSPI central monitoring

**P-13 · Pre-emptive SLA escalation · Tier 2 · Mislabelled today, and now the incumbent owns the retrospective half**
Pendency review is monthly and retrospective; `diffDays > 45` tells you about a breach that has already happened. **Part F item 6 confirms e-SAKSHI already generates the "recommendations pending beyond 45 days" report**, so the retrospective version has no competitive value whatsoever — building it is building their feature. The forecast *is* the entire product here: predicting *which files will breach* from district processing history, so escalation lands while the deadline can still be met. Part F item 9 supplies the evidence that this is a real unmet need — the Standing Committee records **significant delays in project sanction persisting despite** the end-to-end digital system. A count has not fixed it. Two additions make this genuinely yours rather than theirs: **separate rejections from expiries** (B22 — the guidelines allow rejection within 45 days, so a timely rejection is compliance, and A6 shows nobody publishes the split), and forecast at the *district* level so the intervention is staffing rather than nagging. `SLAPage.tsx:44` already claims to do the forecasting. Build it or rename it.

**P-14 · Data quality and entity resolution · Tier 1 · Gap — and the only pain point MoSPI has documented itself**
Free-text location names, inconsistent agency spellings, duplicate agency records, mis-keyed amounts, transliteration variants. Everything downstream inherits this, and Doctrine 6 already commits you to treating missing data as a data-quality finding rather than evidence of wrongdoing — so building the data-quality layer *completes an existing doctrine* rather than adding a new claim. Entity resolution across agency and location names is standard, well-understood AI, and it is the substrate the rest depends on.

**This one now has an official citation, which makes it uniquely safe to pitch.** Part F item 9 records the Standing Committee noting **discrepancies in constituency maps and names in the digital portal** as a live problem MPs are still facing. That is a data-quality defect in the incumbent system, named in a parliamentary report, in the exact category this pain point addresses. Every other pain point in this list requires you to assert that a problem exists; for this one you can cite the Committee. Lead with it when you need to establish credibility early, and note that constituency-name resolution is also a *prerequisite* for your own district and constituency joins — so it is simultaneously their documented problem and your build dependency.

**P-17 · Implementing-agency registration across PFMS and e-SAKSHI · Tier 2 · Gap, and invisible in every work-level report**
Part F item 8 records that **IA registration and approval run through the PFMS process before the information is transmitted into e-SAKSHI**. So before any work can be executed, an agency must exist and be approved in two systems that are reconciled by transmission rather than shared identity. Three consequences, none of which any work-level report can show. A sanctioned work can sit unstartable because its agency is not yet live in PFMS — a delay that will be recorded against the *work*, and against the DA, with no field anywhere explaining the real cause. The same agency can exist under variant names or duplicate records on either side, which is P-14's problem with money attached. And an agency deregistered or blacklisted in PFMS may still hold live works in e-SAKSHI. The AI content is entity resolution across two registries plus anomaly detection on the transmission lag itself; the presentational value is that you can explain a class of delay that the incumbent's reports structurally cannot attribute. Worth raising in Q&A even if you do not build it — it demonstrates that you read the fund-flow architecture rather than just the workflow diagram.

**P-15 · Natural-language questions over the corpus · Tier 1 · Faked today — fix urgently**
This is what the copilot pretends to do, and the honest version is genuinely valuable: an officer asks *"which works in Nashik have money released past 60% with progress under 20%?"* and gets an answer. The safe architecture is **text-to-SQL against a read-only view, with the generated query displayed to the officer** — so it is auditable, matching Doctrine 7's explainability standard, and it cannot mutate anything, matching Doctrine 1. It is the highest-leverage single change available: it replaces your worst integrity liability with a real capability, using an interface you have already built.

**P-16 · Citizen input triage and the field-truth loop · Tier 1 · Gap**
If citizens can suggest works or report problems, someone must classify each submission, route it to the right district, deduplicate campaign-driven bursts, and — most valuably — **match a citizen's geotagged photo of a non-existent asset against a work already marked complete nearby.** That closes the loop between the ledger and the ground, which is the entire premise of the scheme's transparency reforms. Handle with care: this is exactly the surface where Doctrine 4's whitelist and the appeals mechanism the research rightly flags both matter.

## 6. PART E — Do not call these AI

Say "AI" about any of these and you invite a question you cannot win. All are worth building; none is AI.

Geotag EXIF validation is metadata parsing. SLA day-counting is date subtraction, however it is labelled. SC/ST quota checking is division. Released-versus-progress gaps, cost-versus-sanction variance, missing-UC detection and stall-day thresholds are all comparisons. The audit chain is SHA-256, and Doctrine 9 already states the honest claim well — *"retroactive edits cannot be silent,"* not immutability. Perceptual image hashing is computer vision but not machine learning; call it that and you are safe. The MAD z-score is **robust statistics** — and this is the one to be proudest of, because Doctrine 7 chose it deliberately over IsolationForest for explainability. Present that as a considered engineering judgment, which it is. Finally, R-018's "predictive delay velocity" is a linear-expectation ratio; the arithmetic is honest and only the word "predictive" is not.

Part F adds a second, sharper filter on top of that one. There is now a category worse than *not AI*, which is *not AI and not new*: the 45-day SLA report, incomplete-beyond-period (R-006, R-018) and no-payment-for-an-extended-period (R-014) are all reports e-SAKSHI already generates for its users. Nothing about building them is wasted — an oversight layer that could not reproduce the incumbent's own outputs would be untrustworthy — but if a judge hears them framed as DRISHTI's contribution and then finds them in the portal, everything else you said loses its warrant. State plainly that you reimplemented the incumbent's checks so that your findings sit on the same footing as MoSPI's, and spend the pitch on the things Part F shows nobody is doing. One correction to the paragraph above, incidentally: SC/ST quota checking is division, but `quota.ts` is currently performing the **wrong** division, so it does not yet qualify even as honest arithmetic.

## 7. PART F — Primary-source answers (Verified & Reconciled)

### 1. What do the current MPLADS Guidelines say about fund flow, the 45-day clause, completion timeline, inspection percentage, and SC/ST allocation?
The revised MPLADS Guidelines effective from 1 April 2023 moved MPLADS to the new e-SAKSHI-based fund-flow system. The guidelines require the District Authority to process an MP's recommendation and sanction or reject it within **45 days** from receipt of the recommendation. The normal completion period for a sanctioned work is **generally not more than one year** from the date of sanction, subject to the provisions of the guidelines for exceptional situations. District Authorities are responsible for monitoring works and are required to inspect **at least 10% of works under implementation every year**. The guidelines also retain the requirement that MPs recommend at least **15%** of their annual entitlement for SC areas and at least **7.5%** for ST areas.

*Source:* MoSPI, MPLADS Guidelines, revised with effect from 1 April 2023; Standing Committee on Finance reports describing the implementation of those guidelines.

The MPLADS Guidelines contain an indicative list of eligible and ineligible works, with eligibility determined by the principles and conditions of the scheme rather than by a single keyword-based classification. The eligible/ineligible annexure therefore functions as the authoritative rule source for proposal screening.

*Source:* MoSPI, MPLADS Guidelines, revised with effect from 1 April 2023, including the eligible/ineligible works provisions.

### 8. What is the current fund-flow model involving e-SAKSHI, PFMS and banking infrastructure?
The parliamentary material records that MPLADS adopted the **TSA Hybrid / Model 1A** fund-flow arrangement from **1 April 2025**.
